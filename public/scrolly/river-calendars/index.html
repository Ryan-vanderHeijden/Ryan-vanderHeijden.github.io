River Calendars

Every river keeps a calendar. Some swell with snowmelt in June, some with winter storms in January, and some barely change all year. This picture holds that calendar for U.S. rivers, one loop of light per streamgage, each averaged over at least 20 years of daily records.

Scroll to learn how to read it.

Start with one river

The Yellowstone River at Corwin Springs, Montana, just north of the national park. Average every January 1, every January 2, and so on through decades of daily records, and you get its typical year.

The dashed line is the river's mean flow, cubic feet per second. Most of the year runs below it. Then snowmelt lifts the river to about its mean in June.

The vertical scale is logarithmic: each gridline up doubles the flow.

Roll the year into a circle

Now curl the line up so that January 1 sits at the top and the months run clockwise. The dashed mean line becomes a circle. High flow bulges outside it and low flow tucks inside.

December now runs straight into January, so the year has no edges and the June snowmelt pulse becomes one big lobe.

Each river draws its own shape

Coastal Oregon's Nehalem River peaks with winter rain. Maine's Fish River waits for the spring thaw. Texas's Leon River jumps with late-spring storms, and Florida's Fisheating Creek fills in the summer wet season.

Etonia Creek in north Florida barely changes through the year, so its loop is almost a perfect circle.

Color marks when the water comes

Think of each day's flow as an arrow pointing to that day on the clock face. Add them up and the total points to the river's flow-weighted mean date, drawn as the arrow in each loop.

The arrow's length shows how concentrated the year is: long for one dominant season, close to zero for a steady river. Each loop takes the color of its date on the ring.

Now all rivers at once

Here is every gage with at least 20 years of record, stacked on top of each other and drawn in order of when their water arrives. Where many rivers share a rhythm, the light builds up.

Lines are not sized by river: a creek and the Mississippi each count once.

The snowmelt rivers

gages have a flow-weighted date between mid-May and mid-July. About of them sit in the West, where the winter's water waits in the mountains as snow. Many of the rest are Great Plains rivers that peak with late-spring thunderstorms.

The winter-water rivers

gages center on December through February. The Pacific coast is here, where storms arrive in winter and summers are dry. So are many rivers of the Southeast and the Ohio Valley, where cool months mean less evaporation and wetter soil.

The rivers that run all year

Only gages have almost no seasonal swing (arrow length under 0.15). Something stores water for them and releases it slowly: groundwater, a lake or a reservoir upstream.

Put each river back on the map

Each loop shrinks and moves to its gage. The outlines are the 18 water-resource regions of the lower 48 (HUC2 basins), drawn whole, so the Columbia, Great Lakes and Rio Grande basins reach into Canada and Mexico.

Bundle the neighbors

Gages within the same km hexagon now share one center. The regions come out clearly: winter-rain Pacific Northwest, snowmelt Rockies, April-peaking Midwest, a late-winter East, and a Florida that waits for summer rain.

Hexagons with a single gage are left out; those with fewer than five are dimmed.

Explore

Hover over or tap a hexagon to see its gages. Point at the color ring to pick out rivers by when their water arrives.

About the data

Daily discharge from USGS NWIS for every gage with at least 20 years of record. Each gage's mean flow for every day of the year is smoothed over 15 days and divided by the gage's annual mean. A loop's distance from its center is 1 + 0.22·log₂(flow / mean), so the dashed circle is mean flow and every doubling adds 0.22.

The flow-weighted mean date and its strength are the direction and length of the sum of daily flows written as unit vectors around the year, divided by total flow. Gage locations come from GAGES-II; basin outlines from the USGS Watershed Boundary Dataset. Colors use the cyclic map.
